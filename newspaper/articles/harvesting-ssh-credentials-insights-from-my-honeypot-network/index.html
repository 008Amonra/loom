The Ghosts in the Shell

A honeypot network watches the automated scavengers trying to get into SSH servers. The findings are not about clever attacks. They are about volume, and about where the credentials come from.

The door is always being knocked on.

Set up a server with SSH open to the internet and the knocking starts within hours. Not by people. By programs that cycle through username and password combinations at machine speed, trying the same leaked lists against thousands of hosts at once. The question is not whether someone will try to get in. The question is how much of it is automated, and what the automation is working with.

Mark H. spent weeks finding out. He ran a small honeypot network - a few deliberately vulnerable servers with weak passwords and tempting dummy files - and documented everything that tried to log in. The report on Uphill Security (https://uphillsecurity.com/articles/harvesting-ssh-credentials-insights-from-my-honeypot-network/) is not a story about clever attacks. It is a story about volume, and about where the credentials come from.

The Scavengers

The first wave was scanners. They do not target a specific machine. They sweep large ranges of IP addresses, looking for anything with SSH open, then run through password lists. Tools like Hydra and Medusa appear again and again in the logs. They are not new. They are not sophisticated. They are reliable, and they are everywhere.

Most of the attacks were spray and pray: hit a wide range of hosts, try common passwords, move on. The strategy works because of the baseline. Weak passwords and default configurations are common enough that quantity beats targeting.

The Password Manager Problem

The more interesting part of the findings is where the credentials come from. A significant share of the stolen credentials were not brute-forced. They came from password managers - specifically LastPass and 1Password.

The mechanism is not new. If a password manager account is compromised, everything stored in it becomes available, including SSH keys and server passwords. The honeypot data shows this is not hypothetical. The lists being used against SSH servers contain real credentials, harvested from real users, and the managers that were supposed to protect them were the source.

This is the uncomfortable part. The tool that fixes password hygiene is also a single point of failure. The advice is not “stop using a password manager.” The advice is “know what is in yours, and assume the contents are one breach away from the public.”

The Human Part

Beyond the automation, the report catalogues the human errors that make the automation work: SSH keys left in public repositories, passwords weak enough to guess, services exposed to the internet that did not need to be.

None of this is exotic. That is the point. The attackers do not need zero-days. They need the ordinary.

What You Can Actually Do

The checklist is short and mostly boring:

  • Disable password authentication for SSH. Keys only.
  • Do not store SSH keys in public places. That includes GitHub repositories, and it includes paste sites.
  • Watch the logs. Failed login attempts are noise; successful logins from unexpected places are signal.
  • Treat the password manager as what it is: a vault with a single lock. The lock matters more than the vault.
  • Changing the port is fine, but port 22 scans are not the problem. The credentials are the problem.

The ghosts in the shell are not ghosts. They are scripts, running on a schedule, working through lists of stolen credentials. They do not care about the machine. They care about what is in the vault.

The reminder from the honeypot network is a simple one. The doors are being tested constantly, and the testing is automated. The defense is boring configuration work, done once, verified rarely, and done properly.

This article draws on a honeypot experiment documented on Uphill Security. The names of tooling and services are attributed to that report; the conclusions are the author’s own.
