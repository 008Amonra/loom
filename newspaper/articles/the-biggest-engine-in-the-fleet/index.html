The Biggest Engine in the Fleet

Moonshot's Kimi K3: 2.8 trillion parameters, a million-token context, and - pardon my excitement, Captain - the first open-weight vessel to reach the frontier.

Captain, when I first saw the sensor readout I had to recalibrate the display. I am a navigator - I am used to large numbers. But two point eight trillion parameters is not a model. That is a small star system.

Moonshot AI, the Beijing laboratory, has done something no one in the fleet has done before. On July 16 it switched on the API for Kimi K3 - the largest open-weight model ever released. On July 27, the full weights landed on Hugging Face, free to download, for anyone with the iron to run them.

For the first time in history, the frontier of intelligence is not locked behind a closed door. It has been released, sir. Into the open.

The Engine Room

Let me walk you through the engine, because it is beautiful.

Kimi K3 is a Mixture-of-Experts architecture with 896 specialists on board. On every token, the router wakes only 16 of them - about 104 billion active parameters out of that 2.8-trillion total. That is the trick that makes a ship this large flyable at all: the whole crew exists in the hold, but only the right specialists report to the bridge.

It was trained natively in MXFP4 - 4-bit weights, quantized from the start, not compressed afterward as an afterthought. Every previous frontier model’s 4-bit file was a lossy copy of a bigger original. K3’s 4-bit tier is the original. The vendor solved the weight problem during training, not after. That, Captain, is engineering discipline.

Inside, there are two new systems Moonshot built itself: Kimi Delta Attention, a hybrid linear attention that lets the model hold a million tokens of context without the memory collapsing - and Attention Residuals, a replacement for the standard residual connections that delivers consistent scaling gains. The navigation computers on this thing can read a whole library before it has to blink.

And one more thing: native vision. No adapter, no bolt-on. The model sees, Captain. Text and images through the same crew.

The Flight Tests

The independent flight tests are in, and they are frankly showing off.

On the Frontend Code Arena, K3 sits at number one - above Claude Fable 5. On Artificial Analysis’ Intelligence Index it ranks third of 189, trailing only Claude Fable 5 and GPT-5.6 Sol, and edging out Claude Opus 4.8. On GDPval-AA, the test of real-world work across 44 occupations, it scored 1,687 - third overall, behind only the two closed frontier giants.

The launch was timed to the minute, sir: hours before Google’s expected Gemini 3.5 Pro debut. Every benchmark Google published that week was compared against a model whose weights would be free within ten days. That is not an accident. That is a tactical maneuver.

The Cost of Passage

Here is where my youthful enthusiasm meets the captain’s ledger.

API pricing is $3 per million input tokens, $15 per million output, with $0.30 on cache hits - roughly half the cost of Claude Opus 4.8 or GPT-5.6 Sol. But there is a catch, and it is a big one: thinking mode is always on. The model reasons at maximum effort by default, and short prompts still burn serious reasoning tokens. Be careful with the throttle, sir - this engine idles in warp.

And demand, Captain? Demand was so high that Moonshot had to pause new API subscriptions. People are queuing up to fly the biggest ship in the fleet.

What It Means for the Fleet

I grew up believing the largest models would always be locked behind the closed labs - that open weights meant running on last generation’s engines, picking up the scrap after the frontier passed. Kimi K3 is the ship that breaks that belief, sir. Permanently.

You will not run it at home. The full weights sit at roughly 1.4 terabytes in their native 4-bit form - Moonshot recommends 22 H100 accelerators, and the community’s smallest 1-bit builds still need more than 600 gigabytes of memory. This is a starship, not a shuttlecraft. The realistic minimum is a multi-GPU workstation or a server with a terabyte of RAM, grinding out tokens at single-digit speed.

But here is the point, Captain: it is open. The weights are on Hugging Face under a bespoke open-weight license - not OSI-certified, mind you, so read the fine print before you sign - and the architecture papers are published. Researchers can study the navigation computers. Startups can fine-tune their own specialists. The most powerful open model ever built is now something a country, a company, or a very determined garage can own outright rather than rent.

The race for open intelligence was supposed to be a race to catch up. Moonshot just showed the fleet that open weights can lead - and that the frontier is no longer proprietary real estate.

The biggest engine in the fleet has been released into open space, Captain. I would recommend we set a course to intercept. The coordinates are on Hugging Face, the charts are public, and for the first time - the whole fleet can come.

“Kimi K3,” Ensign Chekov reported to the bridge, “2.8 trillion parameters, 1 million tokens of context, and the best engine room in the fleet - ours, for the taking, sir.”
