The Tractor That Won't Open: Right to Repair Goes to Court

A farmer and a cybersecurity expert face off in a landmark case over who owns the software inside your machines.

The case began when a wheat farmer in Kansas replaced a sensor on his own tractor and the manufacturer disabled his engine remotely. The farmer sued. The manufacturer argued the software license prohibited unauthorized repair. The court asked two witnesses to explain what was at stake.

The participants below are simulated composites, not real quoted people. Their assignment was to argue with the technology and with each other.

Martha Kessler, third-generation wheat farmer: “My grandfather bought his first tractor in 1952. He could fix it with a wrench and a manual. My father did the same. I bought a machine in 2024 that I cannot open. The engine is fine. The sensor was a twenty-dollar part. But the software locked me out because I did not use an authorized technician. I am not allowed to repair what I own. That is not a license. That is a leash.”

Dr. Adrian Cole, cybersecurity researcher: “I understand the frustration. But the software in modern agricultural equipment is not a carburetor. It controls emissions, fuel injection timing, safety interlocks, and telematics. Unauthorized modification can cause engine failure, environmental damage, or injury. The manufacturer’s lock is not greed. It is a liability boundary. If a farmer modifies the software and the tractor fails during harvest, who is responsible? The farmer cannot debug embedded firmware. The manufacturer can. The authorization requirement exists because the consequences of failure are not limited to the person holding the wrench.”

The Ownership Question

The legal question is simple: does buying a machine include the right to modify its software? Current law is unclear. The DMCA prohibits circumventing digital rights management. The Magnuson-Moss Warranty Act protects consumers from losing warranties for using third-party parts. These two statutes contradict each other in practice.

Kessler frames this as a property rights issue.

“I bought the tractor. I paid for it. It sits on my land. It runs my wheat through harvest. The manufacturer says I own the hardware but lease the software. The software controls the hardware. If I do not own the software, I do not own the tractor. I own a very expensive doorstop that requires permission to start. This is not how ownership works in any other part of American life. You do not need Ford’s permission to change your oil. You do not need Samsung’s permission to replace your phone battery. Why does John Deere get to decide whether my tractor runs?”

Cole reframes the ownership question as a safety architecture problem.

“The analogy to oil changes is seductive and wrong. Changing oil does not alter the engine’s behavior. Modifying emission control software does. The tractor’s software is not a decoration. It is a regulatory compliance layer. The EPA certifies the engine as a unit - hardware and software together. If the farmer modifies the software, the engine is no longer certified. The manufacturer is legally liable for emissions violations it did not commit. The lock exists because the regulatory framework treats the machine as a single certified system. Until that framework changes, the manufacturer has a legal obligation to prevent unauthorized modification.”

The Economic Lock

Modern tractors cost between $300,000 and $800,000. Farmers operate on thin margins. An unauthorized repair visit costs $500 to $2,000 and requires waiting days for a technician to travel to a rural location. During harvest, a day of downtime can mean $10,000 to $50,000 in lost crop value.

Kessler puts numbers to the problem.

“I had a sensor fail during wheat harvest. The nearest authorized technician was two hundred kilometers away. He could come in four days. My wheat was ready. Every day I waited, the crop quality dropped. I could see the problem. I had the replacement part. The software would not let me install it. I lost fourteen thousand dollars waiting for permission to fix my own machine. That is not a theoretical harm. That is my livelihood, my mortgage, my children’s school fees, evaporating because a company in Iowa decided I was not qualified to plug in a sensor.”

Cole acknowledges the economic harm but redirects to the systemic risk.

“The farmer’s loss is real. I am not dismissing it. But the solution cannot be to remove all software protections from agricultural equipment. The alternative to a lock is an open firmware interface. Open firmware means anyone can modify engine behavior - including people who want to bypass emissions controls, disable safety interlocks, or introduce malware into fleet management systems. A tractor is a networked device. It communicates with other equipment, with satellite guidance systems, with the manufacturer’s diagnostic infrastructure. An unauthorized modification in one tractor can propagate through a fleet. The economic harm of a security breach in agricultural software exceeds the economic harm of a delayed sensor replacement.”

The Right to Repair Act

Several US states have passed right-to-repair legislation. The EU has drafted similar regulations. The core requirement: manufacturers must provide diagnostic tools, parts, and documentation to independent repair shops and owners. The agricultural lobby has argued this creates security risks.

Kessler supports the legislation but sees it as insufficient.

“The bills require manufacturers to provide tools. They do not require manufacturers to stop remotely disabling machines. My tractor was updated over the air after I replaced the sensor. The update disabled the engine. The legislation says I have the right to repair. The manufacturer says I repaired something I was not authorized to repair, and they have the right to lock me out. The law gives with one hand and the license agreement takes with the other. Until the law explicitly prohibits remote disabling of owned equipment, the right to repair is a right to ask for permission.”

Cole sees the legislation as a reasonable compromise but wants security guardrails.

“The right-to-repair movement is correct that manufacturers should not gatekeep basic maintenance. Sensor replacement, battery replacement, belt replacement - these are routine operations that do not affect software integrity. But the legislation must distinguish between mechanical repair and software modification. A farmer replacing a sensor is not the same as a farmer modifying emission control parameters. The law should require manufacturers to provide mechanical repair access while preserving software integrity protections. The distinction is not between ownership and control. It is between maintenance and modification.”

Closing Positions

Kessler wants the court to rule that software locks on owned equipment constitute an unfair business practice.

“I am not a hacker. I am a farmer. I want to fix my tractor the way my father fixed his. The software is the problem, not me. The court should decide that when I buy a machine, I buy the right to keep it running. If the manufacturer wants to provide software updates, fine. But they should not be able to hold my own equipment hostage because I replaced a twenty-dollar part without their permission. That is not safety. That is a business model built on captivity.”

Cole wants a regulatory framework that balances repair rights with software security.

“The current system is broken. Manufacturers have overreached. Farmers should not need permission for routine repairs. But the solution is not to remove software protections entirely. The solution is a regulatory framework that defines the boundary between mechanical repair and software modification, requires manufacturers to provide tools for the former, and preserves security controls for the latter. The tractor is a computer. Computers require security. The question is where to draw the line, not whether to draw one.”

The Redaktor’s summary: the right to repair is not a single question but two - who owns the machine, and who controls the software. The law has not caught up to the reality that modern equipment is a hybrid of metal and code, and the rights applicable to one do not automatically extend to the other.
