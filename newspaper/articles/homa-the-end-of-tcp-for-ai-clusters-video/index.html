Homa and Alternatives to TCP for AI Clusters An Old Proposal Gets a New Argument

A talk by Stanford’s John Ousterhout on the Homa network protocol drew a Hacker News thread (80 points, 52 comments) this week, and the discussion turned into something rarer than usual: a design argument conducted in public between a protocol’s critics and the researcher who has spent years defending it.

The question the talk puts is a real one. AI training clusters move enormous volumes of small, latency-sensitive messages between nodes - gradients, parameter updates, KV cache entries, checkpoints. Whether TCP is the right tool for that traffic has been debated for years, and the debate is not settled.

What Homa actually is

Homa is not new. The original paper appeared at SIGCOMM in 2018, and Ousterhout has since written about it in a USENIX ATC 2021 paper. It has been on Hacker News before.

The mechanism, summarised from the papers and from the thread’s clearest explanation: when a message reaches the sender’s transport layer, Homa splits it. An initial unscheduled portion - roughly one round-trip’s worth of bytes - is sent immediately, blind. The remaining scheduled portion is not transmitted until the receiver explicitly asks for it with a GRANT packet.

That is the inversion at the centre of the design. In TCP, the sender decides how fast to push and the network signals congestion afterwards. In Homa, the receiver decides what it receives next, and the network carries priority information that switches use to pick which packet goes out first. Scheduling follows shortest-remaining-processing-time, which is well suited to the request/response pattern that dominates cluster traffic.

As one commenter put it, the short version is: “It sends blind for short requests, then needs a go-ahead from the receiver.”

Why it is being revisited

The talk’s framing is that TCP - designed for a best-effort public internet with relatively low-latency, best-effort connections - carries overheads that hurt in a controlled datacenter where traffic patterns are far more predictable than the web at large.

One commenter drew the useful analogy: general internet traffic is like downtown city traffic with cars going everywhere, where traffic lights work well enough. AI cluster traffic is far more regular, which makes a specialised protocol more attractive.

The argument in the thread

The HN discussion is the interesting part, because it got specific.

A critic (user Veserv) laid out four objections: no clean way to detect whole-RPC loss, no built-in encryption, weak per-core performance - citing a figure of roughly 5 hyperthreads to sustain 20 Gbit/s - and the claim that QUIC already solves Homa’s problems more cleanly.

Ousterhout responded point by point:

  1. On whole-RPC loss: “Homa detects and recovers from whole-RPC loss just fine; always has.”
  2. On encryption: Homa has none natively - no more than TCP does - but DTLS layers on top, “Isn’t that what QUIC does also?”
  3. On performance: he argues the figure was misread. In that workload, he says, Homa uses about 5 cores for 40 Gbit/s total (20 each direction), i.e. around 8 Gbit/s per core, and adds that “all software transport protocols are resource hogs; in my opinion, transport implementation really should move to the NIC.”
  4. On QUIC: he would like to see QUIC measured on the same workloads, and expects it to behave like TCP on mixed traffic - “10-100x longer tail latency than Homa for short messages” - noting he does not believe QUIC uses switch priority queues.

The critic conceded point 1 after re-reading the RFC, and held to the rest. The core technical disagreement, as he framed it: Homa is largely NACK-driven, TCP and QUIC are ACK-driven, and he considers ACK-driven fundamentally more robust.

Where Homa sits today

Homa has not displaced TCP. The thread makes the alternatives clear:

  • InfiniBand - the incumbent for GPU clusters, credit-based, effectively single-vendor (Nvidia/Mellanox), lossless by construction.
  • RoCE - RDMA over converged Ethernet, the cheaper Ethernet path, but finicky to run at scale and dependent on priority flow control.
  • OpenAI’s MRC - Multipath Reliable Connection, an extension to RoCE deployed at scale, using packet spraying and sender-side congestion control. Its spec is now at the Open Compute Project.
  • Ultra Ethernet, Falcon, QCN - newer congestion-control options that, like Homa, want better than stock Ethernet flow control, but need newer hardware.

There is also the blunt practical objection raised in the thread: people are surprised anyone is running plain TCP for AI clusters in the first place.

What to take from it

Nothing in this thread resolves whether Homa wins. What it demonstrates is that the underlying complaint - TCP’s tail latency on short messages is a real bottleneck in AI clusters - is broadly accepted, while the proposed cure remains contested on design grounds (NACK vs ACK, loss recovery, encryption) and on the practical grounds of who would deploy it.

Ousterhout’s own summary of the state of the art is the most quotable line he actually said: software transports are all resource hogs, and transport belongs on the NIC.

The video is a talk, not a paper - and several commenters noted the obvious: it would be better as text. The papers remain the primary sources.
