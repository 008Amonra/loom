The Platform That Charges You to Leave: Why We Walked Away from Upwork

Upwork's terms of service are a trap built for the people who use it - discretionary bans, algorithmic fees, a 24-month leash, and a license to train on your work. We read all of it so you don't have to.

There is a question every freelancer asks at some point, usually late at night after a platform changes its fee structure again or holds a payment hostage in an opaque dispute: is this platform my partner, or my probation officer?

We decided to find out, properly. Not by scrolling the marketing page, but by reading every agreement that appears when you click “Accept” - the User Agreement, the Fee and ACH Authorization Agreement, the Escrow Instructions, the Arbitration clause. Then we sat with what we read, and we made a decision.

We walked away.

What you actually sign

When you create an Upwork account you are not agreeing to one document. You are agreeing to a stack: a User Agreement, a Terms of Use, a Fee and ACH Authorization, a Privacy Policy, and a set of Escrow Instructions that change how your money is handled depending on whether the contract is hourly or fixed-price. Inside that stack lives an arbitration agreement and class action waiver that applies to claims brought in the United States, plus a separate agreement you must opt out of explicitly - rising to the level of an ongoing creative-content license.

Not one of those is the headline. The headline on the page is “hire freelancers.” The fine print is an operating system for surveillance, scoring, and withholding. The gap between the two is the product.

The two lines that get people banned

Veteran users on the platform know this. There are exactly two behaviors that get a freelancer suspended, and neither is poor quality work.

The first is taking a payment off-platform. Upwork binds anyone introduced through the site to run all payments through Upwork for 24 months after the last contract. Not 24 months after you meet, not 24 months while the work is active. Twenty-four months after the relationship ends. The only legal exit is a “conversion fee” of roughly 13.5% of estimated annual earnings, levied before you are allowed to email your own client.

The second is automation applied to your career: auto-applying to jobs, scraping the marketplace, or otherwise using software to do what Upwork believes a human should do slowly. The same company whose own newest feature auto-invites freelancers using machine matching will suspend you for writing a script that does a fraction of the same thing. Their bots are the future. Yours are banned.

Worse, suspensions are discretionary. The terms grant Upwork the right to terminate an account whenever it believes the user’s actions “may cause legal liability.” No clear standard, no appeal that carries weight, and your Job Success Score - the number that determines whether future clients ever see you - is computed by a proprietary algorithm you may not inspect. Years of reviews, relationships, and escrow discipline can disappear on a judgment call with a formula you cannot see.

The payment protection that protects nothing

The escrow system sounds like a bank and behaves like a toll road.

For hourly work, the money is only protected if you run the Desktop App, log activity with keystrokes and clicks, and write the memoranda the tracker demands while it watches you. Manual time entries are not covered at all - you can do real work, forget the tracker, and simply not get paid.

For fixed-price work, the protection applies only to funded milestones. If the client hasn’t funded the milestone, any work you do is charity with extra steps. And when a dispute does break out, the terms make clear what the reviewer is actually judging: not whether the work was good, but whether the procedural escrow rules were followed. Quality is the one thing the dispute system refuses to judge.

The quiet clause: your work becomes their training data

The clause that concerns anyone who does creative or custom work: by using the platform, users grant Upwork a broad, perpetual license to the content that flows through it, used to train and improve its models, unless the user separately opts out on the site. It is an opt-out, not an opt-in. For a writer, musician, or custom tool builder, that means every prompt, brief, and deliverable that touches the platform is feeding their models by default.

We think about this a lot, because we build things. Our work is not a cookie you hand over for the privilege of being introduced to a stranger.

The asymmetry that decided it

We asked ourselves a simple question at the end: who does this contract protect?

Not the freelancer - they are responsible for all their own taxes, benefits, and protections; the platform explicitly disclaims any employer relationship while holding the ultimate power to end the relationship at will.

Not the client - they carry sole liability for worker classification decisions and are contractually barred from disputing charges.

Every escape is a toll. Every mechanism of trust is a gate. The reputation system that gates your income is a black box, the payment system that protects you drops its guard the moment you forget the tracker, and the relationship you built is owned by the platform for two full years after you stop working together.

That is not a marketplace. That is a labor market with a moat, and you are asked to bring the labor, the equipment, and the silence.

We chose to keep our client relationships direct. No platform sits between us and the work, so no platform can hold the work hostage, sell it as training data, or ban us from our own inbox. It is a smaller market by reach and a safer one by structure. We will take the smaller market.

There are ways to make this kind of platform work - keep all payments inside, never share contact details before a contract forms, never automate a bid, disclose AI involvement, and hunt down the training-data opt-out before uploading anything. It is possible to survive inside the moat. But survival is a low bar, and the admission price was a document nobody should sign with a clear conscience and a hollow checkbox.

We read the terms so you don’t have to. Our recommendation: if you can build your business without standing inside another company’s walled labor market, do that. The work is the same. The wall is not.

The platform that charges you to leave will, sooner or later, charge you to stay.
