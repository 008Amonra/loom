The 22-Minute Signal That Has Been Arriving Since 1988

Somewhere out past the dust, 18,000 light years from here, something is broadcasting on a schedule nobody asked for. It pulses once every 22 minutes — a burst that can last anywhere from thirty seconds to five minutes, arriving in a six-and-a-half-minute window centered squarely on that 22-minute mark. It has been doing this, as far as anyone can tell, since at least 1988. And it has not slowed down by a single tick.

Astronomers using the Murchison Widefield Array in the Australian outback first flagged the source in 2022. Repeating radio signals are not, in themselves, unusual. The night sky is full of them: pulsars, rotating neutron stars, sweep their beams across the Earth like lighthouses, and we have grown used to reading their clocks. The object now catalogued as GPM J1839-10 was meant to be another entry on that long list. Then the astronomers did what good archivists do — they went back through the old records — and the routine story fell apart.

A Clock That Refuses to Slow

The trouble with pulsars is that they should obey physics. A rotating neutron star radiates away angular momentum, and as it does, it slows down. Imperceptibly, over decades, but measurably. The math is unforgiving. GPM J1839-10 rotates once every 21 minutes and 58 seconds. That alone is remarkable — most known pulsars spin in seconds or milliseconds; a 22-minute rotation period is already at the edge of what theory tolerates. But when the team averaged the signal across the full 35 years of archived observations, they found something that should not exist: the rotation period had not changed. Not a little. Not within error bars. At all.

A neutron star spinning once every twenty-two minutes and refusing to decelerate over nearly four decades is not a subtle anomaly. It is the kind of result that makes a review panel re-read the methods section three times. If this object is a neutron star, it is a neutron star unlike any model we have — one that is somehow replenishing the very energy it is supposed to be spending, or one whose nature we have mislabeled entirely.

The Other Explanations Are Worse

The obvious alternative — that the source is not a neutron star at all — is not comforting. The period is too long, the stability too total, the duration too variable for the standard pulsar family photo. Every easy explanation contradicts at least one hard measurement. What survives is the uncomfortable possibility that we are looking at a class of object that barely registers in our catalogs, a kind of stellar corpse that turns over so slowly we had never noticed one because we were not built to sit still long enough to watch.

That is the part that should give anyone pause. The signal has been arriving at Earth since before the fall of the Berlin Wall. For decades it sat in the archives, unremarkable, waiting for a telescope with enough patience and a team with enough curiosity to notice that the clock did not behave. The overlooked web is not only made of forgotten webpages and abandoned APIs. Some of it is out there, in the literal dark.

What the Breakfast Desk Noticed

The 45DGOF8 morning signals desk keeps a running table of such transmissions — a small card on the breakfast page that now reads PERIOD: 22 MIN · STABLE SINCE 1988. It was written as a curiosity, a good-morning fact. But the more the astronomers who found GPM J1839-10 looked, the less it behaved like a fact and the more it behaved like a question that had been quietly waiting its turn.

No one is saying what the source is. The paper trail, such as it is, ends in the Murchison data and thirty-five years of silence — or rather, thirty-five years of punctual, untiring repetition. Whether it is a strange star, a new physics, or simply a clock we do not yet know how to read, the signal is a reminder that the universe does not wait for our categories to catch up. It keeps its appointments regardless. Every 22 minutes.
